# The synthetic marketplace agreed with us. Real postings didn't.

*What 60,000 real Upwork job posts revealed about an agent that scored well on its own test
data. Every number here is reproducible from `evals/results/` in this repository.*

GigBid is a multi-agent copilot for freelancers. LangGraph agents read job posts, judge the
client, decide whether to bid, draft a proposal grounded in the freelancer's portfolio, and
stop for human approval before anything is sent. It was built and tuned against GigSim, a
simulated marketplace we generate from a fixed seed: 1,500 jobs and 300 clients, with
planted scams, reposts and prompt injections, and a hidden "truth" for every job.

On GigSim the system looked healthy:
- the scam rules caught every planted scam with no false alarms;
- the deployed retriever beat plain dense search by seven points of Recall@5;
- the decision step never bid on a job it should have skipped.

The surprising result came when we ran the same system on real data. The parts we had
measured most carefully held up. The parts we had never thought to question broke in ways
no synthetic test could have shown, because the synthetic data was generated by the same
assumptions as the code.

## The rule that was right 37 times out of 37

The Risk Assessor has a handful of hard rules, such as "payment outside the platform" and
"asks for an ID photo", that skip a job without asking the model. One of them was a regular
expression matching the word *crypto*. On GigSim it looked perfect: it fired on 37 of the
planted scam jobs and on none of the clean ones.

We loaded 59,735 real postings from a public Kaggle scrape of Upwork (Apache 2.0) into
GigSim's "real" mode and ran a real job through the full graph. It was an $8,000 contract to
build a volatility model for crypto markets, from a client with a verified payment method.
The agent skipped it as a scam.

Across the real postings the rule fired 625 times. Nearly all of them were legitimate
blockchain, trading and Web3 projects. In GigSim, "crypto" only ever appeared inside a scam
line, because that is the only place our generator put it. Real clients write "crypto"
because their product is about crypto.

The fix was to require a payment phrase: "paid in crypto", "payment via bitcoin", "pay you
directly". On the real postings the rule now fires 13 times. It still catches the same 37
synthetic scam jobs, and the hits that remain are mostly product features ("users pay with
crypto"), which the model step then weighs.

A related rule had a mirror-image problem. We had considered flagging "outside of Upwork".
In real postings, that phrase almost always appears in a *warning*: "do not contact me
outside of Upwork". A rule tuned on synthetic scams would have flagged the honest clients
who were warning against the scam.

Neither rule was wrong about GigSim. Both were wrong about the world, and the synthetic test
set could not tell the difference.

## Prices that were 30 to 100 times too high

GigSim's budget generator multiplied a plausible weekly workload by a plausible hourly
rate. It never asked what clients actually post. We compared it against 2024 Upwork
postings and 1.3 million completed contracts, both public Kaggle sets under ODC-By:

| Category | Real median fixed budget | Real median paid per contract | GigSim median fixed budget |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Machine learning | $150 | $300 | $8,235 |
| Full stack | $150 | $570 | $6,070 |
| Data | $50 | $161 | $5,320 |

GigSim's fixed budgets are 30 to 100 times the real posted ones, and its hourly rates are
about twice as high.

This mattered more than it sounds. The pricing node had a fixed-bid floor of $300, which
sounds modest. On GigSim it never came into play. On real jobs it sat above the median
posted budget in *every* category. On 150 sampled real fixed-price jobs, the floor set the
price 84% of the time, and 57% of the bids were above what the client had posted.

The same gap showed up end to end. On 24 held-out real jobs, 20 ended as skip or watch.
The decision rules, client-risk weights and pricing were all calibrated on a marketplace
where every client was comparatively rich.

The floor is the freelancer's own setting, and refusing underpaid work is a feature. But we
had chosen its default against fictional prices, and nothing in our evaluation could have
told us. We lowered the default to $100. That still sets the price on 31% of the same jobs,
but now in the cases where the client's budget really is too small.

## A skill called "R"

The third failure was smaller and more embarrassing. The Fit Matcher sends one portfolio
search for the job post and one for each required skill. The search tool, sensibly, rejects
queries shorter than three characters.

GigSim's skill vocabulary was written by us: "PostgreSQL", "scikit-learn", "Power BI". Real
Upwork skill tags include "R", "C" and "C#", along with "CV", "XD" and "5G". On the
held-out run, three of the eight data-analysis jobs failed outright: one rejected query for
"R" took down the whole matcher node, and with it the job.

The fix is one line: short skill names stay in the job-post query instead of getting their
own search. The rerun had none of these failures. But it is a good illustration of the
general point. A schema constraint that is invisible on generated data becomes a crash on
the first real input.

## What held up

The failures above are about assumptions baked into rules, thresholds and validation. The
parts of the system that were *learned from evidence* transferred much better.

**Retrieval.** We built a held-out retrieval set from a different Upwork scrape (2024, 50k
postings), labelled by the same rule as our synthetic dev set. On those 469 real queries:

| Variant | Recall@5 | MRR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dense only | 0.480 | 0.491 |
| Hybrid BM25 + dense | 0.521 | 0.516 |
| Outcome prior | 0.568 | 0.572 |
| **Deployed (hybrid + outcome)** | **0.578** | **0.583** |

The deployed pipeline's gain over dense search was larger on real text (+9.8 points) than
on the synthetic set it was chosen on (+7.1). Real job posts are messier, and exact skill
matching via BM25 helps more when the embedding model has more noise to blur. The design
choice we had made "on evidence" survived contact with the real thing.

**The decision step's caution.** On 60 synthetic development jobs the agent never bid on a
job it should have skipped (bid precision 1.00). Its recall was the weaker side (0.69). In
9 of the 11 missed bids, every required skill was present in the retrieved evidence; the
matcher simply scored fit conservatively. That is the right direction to fail in for a
tool whose output a human must approve.

## What didn't transfer at all: scam detection

We also tested the Risk Assessor on 44,042 labelled real postings from a fake-job dataset.
Its hard rules found almost nothing: precision 0.017 and recall 0.002.

The reason is the most honest finding of the exercise. Those fakes are *employment* scams
("earn 5000 a week, contact now"). Our rules were written for *freelance* scams: crypto
payment, off-platform chat, unpaid samples. With the model step added, F1 on a balanced
sample of 58 postings was 0.42, and 0.60 on an earlier run with the same inputs. The model
caught the templated fakes and missed nearly all the subtle ones.

We did not tune the rules to this dataset. It is the wrong population for a freelance
marketplace, and fitting to it would only have bought a better number. The finding stays
in the data card as a known limitation.

## The pattern

The failures share one shape: a value that the synthetic data could not vary was treated as
a fact.
- GigSim's generator decided where "crypto" appears, so the rule learned the generator.
- GigSim's generator decided what budgets look like, so the price floor was calibrated to
  the generator.
- We wrote the skill vocabulary, so the schema matched our vocabulary.

Each test passed because the test and the code shared an author.

The measured components were the ones that held: the retriever choice, chosen by ablation;
the claim verifier; the decision thresholds, which err toward caution. Synthetic data was
good at checking those, because their quality is relative. Variant A beats variant B on
the same queries whoever wrote them. It was useless for checking absolute assumptions about
the world, because it *is* our assumptions about the world.

What we would do differently:
1. **Real data before thresholds.** Load a slice of real data before choosing any
   hard-coded threshold, rule or default, not after. Real data is now the held-out set in
   our latency and cost evaluation, and the place we will find the next surprise.
2. **Mark the synthetic fields.** Every record built from real data carries `_source` and
   `_synthetic` fields, so a number computed on a mix of real and generated fields can say
   which it rests on. GigSim still invents client hire rates and reply preferences that no
   public dataset has.
3. **Treat "perfect on the test set" as a question.** 37 out of 37 should have been the
   first warning sign, not the reassurance it felt like.

*Datasets: Upwork job postings 2023 (Apache 2.0), Upwork jobs 2024 and freelance contracts
(ODC-By 1.0). Attribution and preprocessing are in [DATA_CARD.md](DATA_CARD.md).*
